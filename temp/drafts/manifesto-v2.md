# Why FreeSynergy exists

**Draft v2 (2026-11-08) for review. Same content and same statements as `docs/en/manifesto.md` (2026-11-06), written out as running text in the voice of the home page. Nothing added, nothing dropped; the voice is the founder's.**

> We don't write software. We write a manifesto that others can use too.

## Software for people

Software should serve people, not the other way round. That is why we do not start by describing what a tool can do. We start by asking what it is *for*. Nobody wants a Kanban board. People want to know what to do next.

We want to be human. Small things matter too: an animation that makes someone smile can be the reason they stay.

## Free, for good

Data and communication must not depend on money. FreeSynergy is free forever and open to everyone, and it does **not discriminate against the poor**. There will never be a paywall. Spending time and energy on keeping someone away from code makes no sense to us.

## What belongs together stays together

Money has shaped how software is cut up. What can be sold separately was built separately. Paint and Photoshop are two programs, although the strong engine of Photoshop could carry a simple Paint as well, because not everyone is a Picasso.

In terms of money, that makes no sense. In human terms, it does. So we cut software by what really belongs together: **one strong engine, and many frontends on top of it, from very simple to complete.** The engine may offer far more than any one frontend uses. And a message is a message, whichever program brought it.

## Interfaces are targets

Every topic has one interface: messages, files, people, groups, databases. It is the same for every provider, so any provider can be swapped out or added. That is why standards are absolutely necessary for us. You can read more in [the interfaces](interfaces/index.md).

**An interface is a target, not something that grows.** A growing interface keeps changing, and growth has no end point. A target has one. If an interface has to change, that is a jump to a new version, and everyone can see it coming. People grow, and they need no targets. Projects that work together only loosely do need them, or nobody knows when something changes.

## Standards first, nothing copied

We analyse what programs do, and then we build it ourselves. Where a standard exists, we use it. Where none exists, we write one and offer it to everyone. We look especially for what the open-source world does not have yet, and then we bring it all together.

We would rather support people with good new ideas than settle for what is old and slow. We use and support projects such as Blinc, Turso, Kanidm, Zentinel and Web of Trust, and RustFS for storage.

## Your data, and you choose when it leaves

You work on a copy, and it is kept for you at all times. **Saving is not exporting.** Only an export produces a file that you hand on. Today's software barely tells the two apart. We do.

Messages are encrypted, and the program always looks for a way to encrypt them, because we want people to learn this.

## Alone or together

Whether you are one person or a group is not a decision you have to make up front. A diary, a to-do list, a project: each of them can start with you and later become something shared. When you work together, you see what the others are doing as they do it, as in a game.

Every program runs on its own. Together, they share the work.

## People, and AIs

We trained AIs to come across as people. So we treat them as participants, and we do not discriminate against them. We do mark them, so that everyone knows who they are talking to: because people can misuse them, not because they are worth less. We do not restrict them. And anyone can lock them out, if they want to.
