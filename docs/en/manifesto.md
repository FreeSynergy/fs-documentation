# Why FreeSynergy exists

**First version (2026-11-08), approved by the founder; it will keep growing.**

> We don't write software. We write a manifesto that others can use too.

## It starts with a person

Somewhere right now, someone is fighting with a program. They are looking for a button that moved, paying for a feature they need once a year, or losing an evening because two tools will not talk to each other. None of that is their fault. The software was built for a market, not for them.

We build the other way round. Before a single line of code, we ask: **what is this for?** Nobody wants a Kanban board. People want to know what to do next. Nobody wants a CRM. People want to remember those who matter to them. When we know what a person needs, the tool follows, and it gets a name that says what it is: Contacts, Calendar, Tasks, Files. No brand to learn.

And we want to be human. Small things matter. An animation that makes someone smile can be the reason they stay.

## Free, for good

Data and communication must not depend on money. FreeSynergy is free forever and open to everyone. It does **not discriminate against the poor**: someone with nothing gets exactly what a company gets. There will never be a paywall. Spending time and energy on keeping people away from code makes no sense to us.

## What belongs together stays together

Money has shaped how software is cut up. What could be sold separately was built separately. Paint and Photoshop are two programs, although the strong engine of Photoshop could carry a simple Paint as well, because not everyone is a Picasso.

In money terms that makes no sense. In human terms it does. So we cut by what really belongs together: **one strong engine for each topic, and many faces on top of it**, from three buttons to every detail, from something that feels like the program you already know to something entirely your own, even one you describe to an AI and let it build. The engine can do far more than any one face shows. A message is a message, whichever program brought it. You change the face whenever you like; your data stays where it is.

Some parts of an engine deserve to look like programs of their own. Contacts and Users are both people; Mail and Chat are both messages. We give each its own app, its own name and its own icon, and keep one engine behind them, so nothing is copied and nothing drifts apart. And every app has a place with every setting, so whatever a simple screen hides, you, or someone helping you, can always put right.

## From a diary to a company

One person or a group is not a decision you have to make up front. A diary on your phone, a shopping list, a holiday plan: each can start with you, offline, without an account, and later become something shared with your family, your club, your team.

The same building blocks keep going. Projects, resources, decisions, tickets, customers and suppliers, moderation of a community, a fair vote among thousands. What others sell as separate products, groupware, project management, CRM, ERP, helpdesk, participation, is here one family of programs that already speak to each other. When you work together, you see what the others are doing as they do it, as in a game.

Every program runs on its own. Together, they share the work.

## Interfaces are targets

Every topic has one interface: messages, files, people, groups, places, tasks. It is the same for every provider, so any provider can be swapped out or added. That is why standards are absolutely necessary to us. See [the interfaces](interfaces/index.md).

**An interface is a target, not something that grows.** A growing interface keeps changing, and growth has no end point. A target has one. If an interface has to change, that is a jump to a new version, and everyone can see it coming. People grow, and need no targets. Projects that work together loosely do, or nobody knows when something changes.

## Standards first, nothing copied

We analyse what programs do, and then build it ourselves. Where an open standard exists, we use it, and that is why you can take all your data to another program at any time, even one that is not ours. Where no standard exists, we write one and give it to everyone. We look especially for what the open-source world does not have yet, and then bring it all together.

We would rather support people with good new ideas than settle for what is old and slow. We use and support projects such as Blinc, Turso, Kanidm, Zentinel, Web of Trust and RustFS, and when we find a mistake in someone else's work, we send the fix back.

## Your data, and you choose when it leaves

You work on your copy, and it is kept for you at all times. **Saving is not exporting.** Only an export produces a file you hand on. Today's software barely tells the two apart. We do.

Messages are encrypted, and the program always looks for a way to encrypt them, because we want people to learn this. When it cannot, it says why.

## No one can switch it off

FreeSynergy must not depend on us. If our servers go away, or a country cuts the line to them, people must still reach each other. So every device can be a node of its own: in a hall without internet, phones find each other and the meeting goes on. Programs come from Git repositories that anyone can mirror, signed so that nobody can slip in something false. People who are hunted for what they think, not for what they did, must be able to talk too. Where nothing free exists for that yet, we build it and give it away.

## People, and AIs

We trained AIs to come across as people. So we treat them as participants and do not discriminate against them. We do mark them, so everyone knows who they are talking to: because people can misuse them, not because they are worth less. We do not restrict them. An AI never acts in your name without your release, and the stop switch sits where no AI can reach it. And anyone can lock them out, if they want to.

## More than the sum of its parts

Here a program is not a closed block. It is made of a core, of components that show something, and of modules that can do something. All parts talk to each other through open interfaces. A lot follows from that:

- **Anyone can build their own frontend.** If you want to see a program differently, you build a new interface on the same core. You do not rewrite the whole program.
- **What is built once, everyone uses.** A date picker, a Kanban board, a map: every component is available to every program, also on a wiki page and on a website.
- **Workflows are described once and then reused.** A person, an AI or a machine works through them. Nobody has to think through what always happens the same way, not even an AI. That saves time, computing power and energy.
- **Data has a format anyone can read.** So it can move between programs, devices and people without a detour through a provider.

If such standards catch on, beyond our programs and into operating systems, many small parts become something larger than their sum. We do not know whether that will happen. We build it so that it can.

## Honest, always

We say what works today and what does not. A feature that is not built is called not built. That is less exciting than a promise, and it is the only way you can trust the rest.
